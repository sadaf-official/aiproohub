## Why Most Agent Websites Sound Identical

Open five agent websites in your market and count how many say some version of "dedicated," "passionate," or "trusted." The copy is written the same way everywhere: the agent describes themselves in adjectives, in the third person, with zero numbers and zero specifics. "Jane Doe is a dedicated professional committed to excellence in every transaction." That sentence could describe a dentist.

The cost is real. A buyer or seller choosing an agent opens three to five sites, spends under a minute on each, and picks the one who feels like a specific human with a track record. Generic copy makes you invisible in that comparison.

Three habits separate converting copy from the pack: specificity (numbers, neighborhoods, timelines instead of adjectives), reader focus (what the visitor gets, not who the agent is), and one promise per page. AI is unusually good at this repair: you feed in your facts, it strips the agent-speak, and you edit the result into your voice.

## The Homepage Hero: One Line That Earns the Scroll

Your homepage hero decides whether a visitor reads on or hits the back button. Most agent heroes fail in one of two ways: they describe the agent ("Welcome to the Jane Doe Real Estate Team") or they promise everything ("Buying, selling, investing, and property management"). Neither earns thirty more seconds.

A converting hero follows a simple formula: who you help, what they get, and one line of proof.

Weak: "Helping you achieve your real estate dreams."
Strong: "I help Austin buyers win homes in multiple-offer situations. 34 buyers closed this year, most below asking overbid."

The hero is not the place to be broad; it is the place to be chosen by the right people.

**The hero rewrite prompt**

> "Rewrite my homepage hero. Current version: [paste headline and subhead]. My specialty: [e.g., first-time buyers in Denver]. My proof: [e.g., 41 buyers closed in 2025]. Rules: headline under 12 words, name who I help and the outcome, no words like passionate, trusted, or dreams. Give 3 versions, each with a one-line subhead that adds proof."

Why it works: it forces the who-what-proof structure and bans the adjectives that blur agent copy together.

## About and Bio Pages That Build Trust

The about page is usually the second most visited page on an agent site, and usually the worst written: third person like a resume, designations no consumer understands, and a close about free time. Nobody hires you because of your free time.

Bios that convert answer three questions: why should I trust you (proof), what is it like to work with you (process), and who are you off duty (one human detail). Write in the first person. "I have sold 212 homes in Round Rock" is a claim a person stands behind; "Jane has sold 212 homes" is a resume line. Translate designations into plain English: "Certified Negotiation Expert, the short version of: I argue for my clients for a living."

**The trust-building bio prompt**

> "Write a 200-word first-person bio for my real estate website. Facts: [name, market, years in business, homes sold, one stat, one specialty]. Personal detail: [one sentence, e.g., bought my first home here in 2014]. Tone: confident and plain-spoken. Rules: no third person, no words like passionate or dedicated, translate designations into plain English. End with one line about how I work: [e.g., I pick up my own phone]."

Why it works: first person plus hard numbers plus one human detail is the structure of bios that convert.

## Neighborhood Pages That Rank and Convert

Neighborhood pages are the hardest-working pages on most agent sites: they pull in relocation buyers from search and prove local expertise. They are also usually the thinnest, a paragraph of "vibrant community" filler under a stock photo.

A page that ranks and converts covers what a relocating buyer wants to know: price ranges, commute times, schools with names not ratings, the feel of the streets, and one honest drawback. That last one is the trust builder: "Traffic on Route 9 adds twenty minutes at rush hour" makes everything else believable.

Structure each page the same way: a quick-stats box, a few hundred words of real description, a short FAQ, and one call to action for a tour or listing alerts.

**The neighborhood page builder**

> "Write a neighborhood guide for my real estate website about [neighborhood, city]. My facts: [median price, home styles, commute times, school names, amenities, one honest drawback]. Structure: 1) 40-word intro naming who this neighborhood fits, 2) quick-stats bullets, 3) 250 words of specific description, no words like vibrant, charming, or hidden gem, 4) 4 FAQ questions a relocating buyer would ask with honest answers, 5) closing paragraph with a call to action for listing alerts."

Why it works: the fixed structure guarantees a complete page, and the honest-drawback requirement turns a brochure into something a buyer trusts.

## Buyer and Seller Pages That Speak to Pain

Most agents cram buyers and sellers onto one "Services" page with two paragraphs of "whether you are buying or selling." That page converts nobody, because buyers and sellers have completely different fears. Split them.

The seller page answers: "Will this agent get me the most money with the least hassle?" Show a pricing process, a marketing list with specifics, and a week-by-week timeline. Sellers hire process, not adjectives.

The buyer page answers: "Will I overpay or get beaten by cash offers again?" Lead with your offer strategy and how fast you move. Buyers hire an advocate with a plan.

**The seller page rewrite prompt**

> "Rewrite my seller services page. Current page: [paste it]. My process: [e.g., pricing from 90 days of closed comps, professional photos and floor plans, launch in 7 days, weekly seller reports]. My stats: [e.g., 98.6 percent of list price, 21 days on market]. Rules: write to the seller's fear of leaving money on the table, describe the process week by week, use my stats as proof, no words like dedicated or passionate. End with a call to action for a free home valuation. 300 words."

Why it works: it reframes the page around the seller's fear and anchors every claim to a stat instead of an adjective.

## Landing Pages for Lead Magnets

Lead magnet pages, home valuation, relocation guide, buyer checklist, have one job: trade something useful for an email address. Conversion lives or dies on the offer's specificity. "Get our free guide" converts at a fraction of "The 2026 Round Rock Seller's Guide: what 212 recent sales tell us about pricing your home."

Three rules. The headline is the offer, not your brand. Three to five bullets name what is inside, each with something specific. The shortest form you can stand: name and email only. Every extra field costs you leads. And give the thank-you page one low-pressure next step, like "browse this week's new listings in your price range."

**The lead magnet landing page prompt**

> "Write a landing page for my free [home valuation report / relocation guide / buyer checklist] for [market]. What is inside: [3-5 specific items, e.g., pricing data from 200 recent sales]. Rules: headline under 15 words stating the offer, 4 bullets describing what is inside, no hype words like ultimate or exclusive, form fields are name and email only, one closing sentence telling them what happens next. Under 200 words. Also write a 2-sentence thank-you page message with one low-pressure next step."

Why it works: it keeps the offer specific, the page short, and the form minimal, the three levers that move lead magnet conversion.

## CTAs That Actually Get Clicked

Your calls to action are the smallest copy on your site and the most clicked. Most agent sites waste them: "Contact Me," "Learn More," "Click Here." Those buttons describe the mechanics, not the benefit. Nobody wakes up wanting to contact an agent; they want the valuation, the tour, or the answer.

The fix is a verb plus the benefit: "Get my home valuation," "See this week's new listings," "Book a 15-minute call." Each one says what happens after the click, which is what actually gets the click. A line under the form, "Takes 60 seconds, no spam, unsubscribe anytime," answers the unspoken objection: "If I fill this out, will I get called forever?" Put one CTA per section, not one per page.

**The CTA rewrite prompt**

> "Rewrite the calls to action on my real estate website. Current buttons: [list them, e.g., Contact Me, Learn More]. For each, give 3 replacements in 'verb + benefit' format (e.g., 'Get my home valuation'). Also write one line of reassuring microcopy for under my lead form, addressing the fear of being spammed. Keep every button under 6 words."

Why it works: verb-plus-benefit buttons convert, and the microcopy answers the objection that kills most agent forms.

## Make It Scannable: Headers, Bullets, Short Paragraphs

Here is the uncomfortable truth: most visitors will not read your copy. They will skim the headers, read the bullets, and decide in thirty seconds. Writing for skimmers is respecting how the web works.

Four rules. Headers that carry meaning on their own: "How I price your home" beats "My Process." Bullets over paragraphs for facts, stats, and steps. Paragraphs of two to three lines. And bold the single most important sentence in each section.

Test every page before publishing: scroll through it in thirty seconds without reading. If you cannot tell what it promises from the headers and bullets alone, rewrite it. AI drafts run paragraph-heavy, so make scannability an explicit instruction in every prompt, or run this final pass.

**The scannability pass prompt**

> "Make this page copy scannable without cutting information: [paste page copy]. Rules: headers state the section's point in under 8 words, convert facts and steps into bullets, no paragraph over 3 lines, bold the most important sentence per section. Keep my voice and all my stats."

Why it works: it turns a readable draft into a skimmable page, and skimmable pages convert visitors who give you thirty seconds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I write my agent website copy in first or third person?**
First person, almost always. "I sold 212 homes" is a claim a person stands behind; "Jane sold 212 homes" is a resume line. Exception: a true team page with multiple agents.

**Will AI-written website copy hurt my Google rankings?**
Google ranks pages on usefulness, not authorship. AI-assisted copy that is specific, accurate, and helpful ranks fine. What hurts you is thin, generic, or duplicated content. Edit every AI draft for accuracy.

**How often should I update my website copy?**
Refresh stats quarterly, they go stale fastest, and rewrite one page a month starting with the homepage hero. A site that improves monthly beats one that was perfect once, three years ago.
