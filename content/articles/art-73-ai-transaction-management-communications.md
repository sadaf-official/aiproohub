## Set the Communication Cadence Before Day One

Clients panic in silence. Tell them at contract signing exactly when they will hear from you, and a Friday update becomes something expected instead of something hoped for. Send a one-page "what happens now" note: a weekly update every Friday by 5 p.m., immediate contact for anything urgent, daily check-ins during closing week. AI drafts it in your voice in two minutes.

**The cadence-setter**

> "I am a real estate agent. Write a one-page 'what happens now' message I send to clients the day their contract is signed. Include: (1) a congratulatory opening, (2) the communication cadence (weekly written update every Friday by 5 p.m., plus immediate contact for anything urgent), (3) the major milestones ahead in plain language (inspection, appraisal, title work, final loan approval, final walkthrough, closing day), (4) what I need from the client this week, and (5) a reassuring closing line. Make a buyer version and a seller version: the seller version focuses on appraisal, repairs, and closing logistics; the buyer version focuses on inspection, lender deadlines, and money. Warm, confident tone, under 400 words each."

Why it works: separate buyer and seller journeys have different worries, so their roadmaps must differ too.

## The Weekly Under Contract Update: Buyer and Seller Versions

The weekly update is the heartbeat of the system. Four sections, no more: what happened, where the file stands, what happens next week, what you need from the client. Feed AI your bullet notes and it drafts the update in five minutes. Read every draft before sending. AI writes the scaffolding; you sign off on the facts.

**The weekly update drafter**

> "I am a real estate agent. Turn these deal notes into a weekly 'under contract' client update: [paste bullet notes: inspections, appraisal, lender status, title, deadlines, open items]. Structure: (1) What happened this week (2-3 bullets), (2) Where we stand right now (one sentence), (3) What happens next week (2-3 bullets), (4) Anything I need from you (numbered list, with dates). Tone: warm, calm, professional. Under 250 words. Do not invent any facts not in my notes. If something sounds like bad news, keep it factual and pair it with the plan."

Why it works: the "do not invent any facts" line is the safety rail for a live legal process. AI must never fill a gap with a guess.

## Milestone Checklists Clients Can Actually Follow

Clients should not have to understand your process. A one-page milestone checklist translates the journey into plain language: what happens, who handles it, when, and what could hold it up. Each milestone shows the client's job in bold, because files move faster when clients know exactly what they owe and when.

**The milestone checklist builder**

> "I am a real estate agent in [state]. Create a one-page milestone checklist for a buyer going from accepted offer to closing day. Include 6 to 7 milestones: inspection, appraisal, title search, loan approval, homeowners insurance, final walkthrough, closing day. For each: one plain-language sentence on what it is, who handles it, typical timing (weeks from contract), and the client's job in bold. End with a box: '3 things that can delay closing, and how we prevent them.' Plain language, no jargon. Flag anything that varies by state law with [CHECK CURRENT]."

Why it works: the bolded client-job format turns passive readers into participants. Files move faster when clients know exactly what they owe.

## Communicating Delays and Bad News Without Causing Panic

Every transaction hits a snag. The mistake is waiting: silence after bad news reads as disaster. The correct move is fast, factual, and forward-looking: what happened, what it means, the plan, what you need from the client. Never soften bad news into vagueness. "The appraisal came in $12,000 low" calms far more than "a small hiccup."

**The bad-news script writer**

> "I am a real estate agent. Help me write a message to my [buyer/seller] client about this problem: [describe the issue factually: low appraisal, lending delay, inspection issue, title problem]. The message must: (1) state what happened in one clear sentence, (2) explain what it means for them in plain language, (3) present 2 to 3 options or next steps, (4) say what I am doing about it right now, (5) say exactly what I need from them and by when. Tone: honest, calm, in control. No jargon, no minimizing, no catastrophizing. Under 300 words. End with a reassuring line specific to the plan."

Why it works: it forbids minimizing and catastrophizing, the two ways agents wreck trust with bad news. The structure does the thinking for you.

## The Closing-Week Communication Blitz

Closing week needs daily contact, not the weekly cadence. Clients are wiring funds and packing boxes, so confirm every detail out loud: walkthrough, what to bring, exact time and location, who to call. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau stresses verifying wiring instructions through a known phone number, never from an email alone ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov)). Put that warning in your own words on every file.

**The closing-week plan**

> "I am a real estate agent. Write my closing-week communication plan for a buyer closing on [day of week, date]. Include a 5-day message schedule (each under 150 words): Monday: final walkthrough details; Tuesday: wire fraud warning (verify by a known phone number, never emailed instructions) plus documents to bring; Wednesday: final numbers review and what the cash-to-close figure means; Thursday: closing day logistics (time, location, who attends, how long it takes); Friday: congratulations message with next steps (utilities, locks, insurance binder). Tone: warm, excited, precise. Mark every item I must confirm with the title company with [CONFIRM]."

Why it works: the [CONFIRM] markers separate what AI can draft from what only the title company can verify. That separation keeps the blitz accurate.

## Build an AI-Generated FAQ for Anxious Clients

Anxious clients ask the same questions on every file: Can I buy furniture before closing? Why does the lender keep asking for documents? What happens at the final walkthrough? Write the answers once with AI, save them as a one-page FAQ, and send it at contract. Every question it answers is a call you never get. Review each answer for your market, refresh twice a year, and share HUD's homebuying resources at [hud.gov](https://www.hud.gov) alongside your own.

**The anxious-client FAQ builder**

> "I am a real estate agent. Write a one-page FAQ for buyers under contract, answering the 12 questions anxious first-time buyers ask most between contract and closing: buying furniture or opening credit before closing, why the lender keeps asking for documents, what a closing disclosure is and when it arrives, what happens at the final walkthrough, what the title company does, what happens if the appraisal is low, what delays closings most often, whether they can back out and lose earnest money, what 'clear to close' means, who attends closing, how long closing day takes, and what happens with their keys. Each answer: 2 to 3 sentences, plain language, honest. Flag anything that depends on state law or current programs with [VERIFY]."

Why it works: answering scary questions proactively signals you have seen it all before. That confidence is the product clients are buying.

## Post-Closing Follow-Up Templates That Build Referrals

Most agents vanish after the keys change hands. Three beats keep the relationship alive: day-after congratulations with practical next steps, a two-week check-in, and a 90-day touch with a maintenance reminder and a soft review ask. Useful first, relational second. National Association of Realtors research shows referrals and repeat business remain the dominant channel ([nar.realtor](https://www.nar.realtor)), and this follow-up is what feeds it.

**The post-closing sequence**

> "I am a real estate agent. Write 3 post-closing follow-up messages for a buyer. Message 1 (day after closing): congratulations, plus 3 practical next steps (change locks, file the homestead exemption if available in [state], set up the insurance binder). Message 2 (two weeks later): a warm check-in plus an offer to help with anything odd. Message 3 (90 days later): a seasonal home maintenance reminder with 4 tasks, plus a soft ask for a review. Each under 150 words, warm and personal, no sales language. Make message 3 easy to customize by season."

Why it works: the sequence is useful first, relational second. The review ask rides on three months of being helpful, which is why it converts.

## Cut the "What's Happening With My File" Calls

Count your "just checking in" contacts per file per week. No system: three to five. Weekly update plus milestone checklist: zero to one. Tell clients at contract: "Check Friday's update first. If it is urgent, text me." Then send it every Friday. AI makes the habit cheap: five minutes of notes in, one polished update out.

**The weekly deal-notes habit**

> "I am a real estate agent who runs a contract-to-close communication system. Every Friday I dictate rough notes about each active file. Create a reusable weekly template per file: fields for client name, property, closing date, this week's progress (3 lines), next week's plan (3 lines), open items with owners and dates (3 lines), client action items (3 lines), and risk flags. Then write instructions for turning it into the weekly client update. Keep it plain and copy-pasteable."

Why it works: it turns a habit into a workflow. Consistent notes make consistently good drafts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often should I update clients who are under contract?**
One written update a week, plus immediate contact for anything urgent. Weekly is the sweet spot: frequent enough that clients never wonder, spaced enough to have real content. Go daily during closing week.

**Should I use AI to write transaction updates to clients?**
Yes, for drafting, never for deciding. AI turns your rough notes into a polished update fast, but you must approve every word before sending. Updates describe a live legal process, so the "do not invent facts" instruction in your prompt is non-negotiable.

**What do I say when the closing is delayed?**
Say it fast, factually, and with the plan attached: what happened, what it means, the options, what you are doing, and what you need from the client. A delay delivered quickly with a plan builds trust. One the client discovers through someone else destroys it.

**How do I handle a client who texts me constantly during the transaction?**
Set the cadence at contract and hold it. Most constant texting comes from uncertainty, not personality, and it usually drops once the weekly update arrives reliably. If it does not, have a kind, direct conversation: "Text me anytime something is urgent. For everything else, Friday's update has you covered."

**Do transaction management systems work for sellers too?**
Absolutely, with a different emphasis. Sellers worry about the appraisal, repair negotiations, and closing logistics. Buyers worry about the lender, the inspection, and the money. Same cadence, different content, which is why the prompts above build separate buyer and seller versions.
