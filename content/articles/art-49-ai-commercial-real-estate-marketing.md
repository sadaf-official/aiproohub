## Why Commercial Marketing Plays by Different Rules

Residential marketing sells a feeling. Commercial marketing sells math. An investor reads your flyer the way an underwriter reads a loan file: scanning for yield, risk, and exit. Clear numbers and honest risk framing are what close them.

That difference changes what AI is for. In residential, AI polishes prose. In commercial, AI is a structuring engine: it takes your rent roll, operating statement, and market notes and organizes them into the formats investors expect. A sloppy executive summary signals a sloppy deal, so the bar for accuracy is absolute.

### What investors actually read

Active CRE buyers read the same shortlist: the one-page executive summary, the financial summary (NOI, cap rate, cash on cash), the lease abstract or rent roll, and the market section. If those four are crisp, the rest of the OM gets read. If they are muddy, the package gets closed.

### Where AI fits, and where it never belongs

AI fits the drafting and structuring: turning your numbers into narrative, formatting sections, drafting outreach, summarizing market data. It never belongs in the numbers themselves. AI should never calculate your cap rate from scratch, invent lease terms, or "estimate" rents to fill a gap. Every figure traces back to a document you can produce: the rent roll, the T-12, the lease. AI arranges the evidence. You supply it.

## Executive Summaries That Open Wallets

The executive summary is the most-read page in commercial real estate, and many investors decide whether to keep reading based on it alone. Its job: make the deal legible in 60 seconds. Price, returns, the one reason this asset matters, and the ask.

AI excels here because the format is rigid. Feed it complete inputs (price, NOI, cap rate, square footage, occupancy, lease summary, your one-line thesis) and it produces the page.

**The one-page deal snapshot**

> "Write a one-page executive summary for a commercial investment offering. Property: [type, city]. Price: $[X]. NOI: $[Y]. Cap rate: [Z]%. Occupancy: [N]%. Key facts: [3-5 bullets: anchor tenant, lease term, improvements, location driver]. Investment thesis: [one sentence]. Format: headline with the returns, 3 short paragraphs (asset, financials, opportunity), 5 investment-highlight bullets. Tone: confident, plain, no hype. End with a call to request the full offering memorandum."

Why it works: the thesis comes first, so the AI writes around your argument, and the rigid format matches what investors scan.

## Turning NOI and Cap Rate Into a Story

Numbers without narrative get ignored; narrative without numbers gets dismissed. The sweet spot is a short paragraph translating the financials into plain English: what the NOI means, why the cap rate is where it is, and how cash on cash looks for a typical buyer. This is the paragraph most brokers skip and most investors want.

Feed AI your actual figures and the comps that contextualize them. Ask it to explain, not to sell. "At a 6.8% cap rate against a 6.1% submarket average, this asset prices in its value-add upside" reads like analysis; "amazing returns you cannot miss" reads like a red flag.

**The financial narrative builder**

> "Write a 150-word financial narrative for a commercial investor package. Verified figures: price $[X], in-place NOI $[Y], cap rate [Z]%, year-1 cash on cash [W]% at [LTV]% leverage, [N]% occupancy. Context: [submarket average cap rate, recent comps, one sentence on the local economy]. Explain in plain English: why the cap rate is set where it is, what drives the cash on cash return, and the biggest assumption behind the numbers. No hype, no invented figures. Flag any figure that needs a footnote."

Why it works: the last line makes the AI flag numbers that need sourcing before an investor asks.

## Offering Memorandums Without the 60-Hour Grind

A proper offering memorandum can eat two full workweeks, most of it assembly, not insight. With source documents in hand, AI can draft every text section of an OM in an afternoon, leaving your time for judgment: the thesis, the risk section, and verification.

Work section by section, not in one giant prompt. Feed the AI the rent roll for the tenancy section, the T-12 for the financial section, your market notes for the location section. Small inputs, verifiable outputs.

**The OM section drafter**

> "Draft the [property description / investment highlights / market overview] section of an offering memorandum for [property, city]. Use only these verified facts; invent nothing: [paste 8-15 facts]. Length: [250-400] words. Tone: professional, factual, for experienced investors. Short intro paragraph, then supporting points. Add [SOURCE NEEDED] after any claim that needs a source document. Do not round numbers."

Why it works: the [SOURCE NEEDED] tag turns verification into a checklist instead of a re-read.

The golden rule: AI drafts, the broker verifies. Check every figure against its source document, hunting the two classic errors: drifted numbers and confident claims with no source.

## LinkedIn Outreach That Gets Replies From Investors

Commercial deals often start with a direct message, not a listing portal. Investors and 1031 exchange buyers live on LinkedIn, and a sharp, specific message beats a blast email every time. Lead with the fit, not the property: why this deal matches what they buy.

AI personalizes at scale without sounding like a mail merge. Feed it the investor's public focus (asset class, markets, deal size) plus your deal's three strongest points.

**The investor outreach message**

> "Write a LinkedIn connection note and follow-up message for a commercial real estate investor. Investor profile: [asset classes they buy, typical deal size, markets]. My deal: [property type, city, price, cap rate, one-line hook]. Connection note: under 300 characters, reference something specific from their profile, no pitch. Follow-up: under 120 words, lead with why this fits their criteria, give the three numbers that matter, end with one low-pressure question. Tone: peer to peer, never salesy. No emojis."

Why it works: the profile-specific opening is what separates a reply from a delete.

## Property Flyers for Office, Retail, and Industrial

Flyers still move commercial deals: emailed as PDFs, handed out at industry conferences, pinned in break rooms. But each asset class reads differently. Office tenants care about floor plates and parking ratios. Retail buyers care about traffic counts and co-tenancy. Industrial buyers care about clear heights, dock doors, and power. One generic template fails all three.

Use AI to write class-specific flyer copy: give it the asset class and the three metrics that class cares about most.

**The asset-class flyer writer**

> "Write flyer copy for a [office / retail / industrial] property. Facts: [address, sq ft, key specs: office = floor plate, parking ratio, class; retail = traffic count, anchors, frontage; industrial = clear height, dock doors, power]. Investment angle: [price, cap rate, occupancy]. Format: headline under 10 words, 3 spec-driven bullet highlights, one 60-word paragraph, contact call to action. Tone: direct, factual, no lifestyle language."

Why it works: naming class-specific metrics stops generic filler and forces spec-driven copy.

## Submarket Research Summaries in an Afternoon

Investors buy submarkets as much as buildings, and the market section is where credibility is won or lost. AI compresses the drafting: you supply data points from CoStar, CBRE reports, or census data, and AI structures them into a narrative of vacancy, rent growth, and absorption.

The discipline: AI summarizes your research, it does not do your research. Every statistic must come from a report you can name, cited inline so the trail is visible.

**The submarket snapshot**

> "Write a 300-word submarket overview for a commercial investment package. Market: [submarket, metro]. Data points with sources: [vacancy rate and trend, asking rent and YoY change, net absorption, 2-3 demand drivers, one development catalyst]. Structure: conditions, trends, outlook. Cite the source after each statistic. If two data points conflict, note the conflict."

Why it works: investors trust a broker who shows their work, including conflicting data.

## Lease-vs-Buy Content for Occupier Clients

Not every commercial client is an investor. Owner-occupiers and expanding businesses face the lease-versus-buy decision, and brokers who educate win the relationship before the transaction. A clear one-pager comparing the two paths, with real local numbers, is one of the highest-converting pieces a commercial broker can publish. AI drafts the framework; you plug in the local figures.

**The lease-vs-buy one-pager**

> "Write a one-page lease-vs-buy comparison for a [business type] considering [X] sq ft in [city]. Lease: [rent per sq ft, term]. Buy: [price per sq ft, down payment %, loan rate]. Cover: monthly cost comparison, 10-year equity build-up, flexibility trade-offs, tax treatment in plain English, who should lease vs buy. Balanced tone, no hard sell. Add: this is general information, not financial advice."

Why it works: balanced framing builds trust, and the disclaimer keeps you on the right side of the advice line.

## The Compliance Layer: Disclaimers That Protect You

Commercial marketing carries real liability: a wrong number in an investor package is a potential claim, and AI writes so confidently that errors look intentional. Build compliance into the workflow, not onto it.

Every AI-assisted package should carry a disclaimer and pass a human verification checklist: financials traced to source documents, projections labeled as projections, fair housing language checked. AI can draft the disclaimer; only you can verify.

A starting disclaimer, reviewed by your broker and attorney before use:

> "The information in this package was prepared to assist prospective buyers and investors. While believed to be reliable, it has not been independently verified. All financial figures, projections, and property details should be independently confirmed by the recipient and their advisors. This material does not constitute investment, legal, or tax advice."

Pair this with the OM verification habit, and AI becomes a speed tool with guardrails instead of a liability with a keyboard.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can AI write a full offering memorandum?**
AI can draft every text section of an OM from your source documents: property description, investment highlights, market overview, and tenancy summaries. It cannot supply the numbers, choose the investment thesis, or verify accuracy. Treat AI as your fastest drafter, never your analyst.

**Is it safe to let AI write investor-facing financial narratives?**
Yes, with one non-negotiable rule: every figure comes from a document you verified, and you re-check each number in the draft against that document. AI explains verified numbers clearly. It is dangerous at supplying numbers, because it will invent them rather than admit a gap.

**How do commercial brokers use AI for investor outreach?**
Feed AI the investor's public criteria plus your deal's key numbers, and it drafts messages that read like homework was done. The brokers getting replies lead with fit, not with the property.

**Does AI-generated commercial marketing comply with regulations?**
Compliance comes from your process: verify all financials, label projections as projections, include proper disclaimers, and have your broker review investor packages. AI does not change your obligations, it just speeds up the drafting.

**What is the biggest mistake brokers make with AI in commercial marketing?**
Letting AI fill gaps in the data. An AI that does not know the lease expiration will write a confident-sounding sentence anyway. If the input is missing, the output gets a [NEEDS DATA] flag, not a guess.
