AI Comparative Market Analysis (CMA) Narrative Writing

Meta: Sellers don't hire the agent with the most comps. They hire the agent who makes the numbers make sense.

Most listing presentations drown in data: six comparable sales, a grid of adjustments, a price range in bold, and a seller whose eyes glazed over at slide two. The agents who consistently win listings learned something years ago: the CMA itself doesn't win the business. The story around the CMA does. That story now has a workflow: AI comparative market analysis (CMA) narrative writing, the practice of using AI to turn your comp grid into plain-English explanation a seller can actually follow. Your numbers, your judgment; AI handles the translation into narrative, script, and adjustment explanations.

Why CMA Narrative Writing Wins the Listing

At every listing appointment, the seller has already checked their home value in three places and anchored on a number, usually 5 to 10 percent above the data. They don't need more data. They need someone to make it make sense and make the price feel safe.

Data alone does not do that. A seller who cannot explain your price cannot defend it: not to their spouse, not at 2 a.m., not when a neighbor says they could have gotten more. The listing presentation is a teaching moment disguised as a sales pitch. The agent who teaches wins.

A CMA narrative is the written or spoken story connecting three things: the subject property, the comps you chose, and the price you recommend. It answers the four questions every seller silently asks. Why these comps and not others? What are these pluses and minuses? What about the low one? And why this price instead of the higher one I want? Answer all four and your CMA presentation to sellers becomes a pricing narrative they can repeat at home. AI comparative market analysis (CMA) narrative writing produces clear, honest answers in minutes. You do the appraising. AI does the explaining.

CMA Narrative Writing With AI: The Workflow

The workflow has four steps, and the order matters. AI never touches step one.

Step 1: You do the analysis. Pick comps, make adjustments, set your price range. Licensed judgment. A beautiful narrative on bad comps is a well-written mistake.

Step 2: Feed AI the raw material. Paste comp details and the adjustment grid. Add the seller context AI can't see: motivation, timeline, the objection you expect.

Step 3: Ask for three outputs. A written narrative for the leave-behind, a spoken kitchen-table script, one-sentence adjustment explanations.

Step 4: Edit for your voice and verify every number. Read the draft aloud. Rewrite any sentence you would never say. Then check every figure against your CMA, because AI writes beautifully and calculates unreliably.

Prompt: Build the full CMA narrative

"You are an experienced listing agent writing a CMA narrative for a seller presentation. Subject property: [beds, baths, square feet, lot, condition, key updates]. Comparable sales: [paste each comp with sold price, sold date, beds, baths, square feet, condition notes]. My adjustment grid: [paste adjustments with dollar amounts]. Target price range: [X to Y]. Seller context: [motivation, timeline, likely objection]. Write three things. (1) A 200-word written narrative in plain English. (2) A 90-second spoken kitchen-table script. (3) One-sentence plain-English explanations of each adjustment. Tone: honest, direct, no hype, no jargon. Do not invent facts."

CMA Narrative Writing: Explaining Adjustments in Plain English

Adjustments are where trust is won or lost. A $12,000 garage adjustment is routine to you; to a seller it looks like moving numbers to get the answer you want. Teach the principle in one sentence: adjustments translate differences into dollars so each sale compares fairly to your home. This comparable sales explanation is where the pricing narrative either earns trust or loses it.

The formula that works every time: name the difference, name the dollar amount, name the direction, in words anyone would follow. "Comp 2 sold for $425,000, but it has a finished basement and yours does not, so we subtract about $15,000 to put it on equal footing." No methodology lecture. No paired-sales terminology at the kitchen table.

Prompt: Plain-English adjustment explanations

"Here are my CMA adjustments: [paste grid with amounts and reasons]. For each, write one sentence for a homeowner with no real estate background: name the comparable sale, the difference, and the dollar adjustment. Under 25 words each. Never use 'paired sales,' 'regression,' or 'methodology.'"

Keep the best phrasing for each line and build a reusable library. Top producers rehearse good lines; they don't improvise.

If an adjustment is debatable, say so: "Pool adjustments here run $10,000 to $20,000; I used $15,000." Sellers trust shown uncertainty over false precision.

Handling Low Comps Without Losing the Seller's Trust

Every CMA has one: the comp that sold low and drags your range down. Burying it on page four never works; the seller has Zillow, Redfin, and a neighbor. If you do not address the low comp, the seller assumes you are hiding something or missed it. Both cost you the listing.

Name it early, contextualize it honestly, move on. Low comps almost always have a reason: distress sale, estate sale, dated interior, busy road, or a sale from a softer market.

Prompt: Address the low comp honestly

"One comp sold well below the others: [details, sold price, date, condition]. Likely reasons: [your notes]. Write 60 words I can say to a seller: acknowledge the sale, give the honest reason it sold lower, explain why it stays in the analysis. Straightforward, respectful, no spin."

The technique underneath is bracketing: "This sale sets the floor. These two set the ceiling. Your home sits here, and here is why." Sellers grasp brackets intuitively. When the low comp has no good explanation, say so: "One unexplained sale does not set the market. Three consistent ones do." Candor about the weak spot makes the rest more believable.

Positioning Your Pricing Strategy

The narrative ends at a strategy, and the strategy must match the story you just told. Listing presentation pricing belongs in the narrative, not just the grid. There are three strategies:

Price at market when the seller needs speed or the data is tight. The narrative says the comps cluster here and pricing here should bring strong activity in the first two weeks.

Price slightly above with a plan when the home has an edge the comps miss: list at X, watch feedback for 14 days, adjust to Y if signals are soft. A test price without an adjustment plan is just an overprice.

Price aggressively for competition when inventory is thin. The narrative explains the psychology: pricing at the low end of the range invites multiple offers, which often land above a single hopeful asking price. Our piece on pricing psychology in listing copy covers why this works.

Prompt: Recommend and defend a pricing strategy

"Based on this CMA [paste comps and range]: inventory [low/moderate/high], days on market [X], seller timeline [timeline], priority [speed/top dollar]. Recommend one: price at market, price above with an adjustment plan, or price aggressively for competition. In 150 words: recommendation, expected outcome, honest risk. Then one paragraph for the seller who asks for $25,000 above my range."

Rehearse your answer to the overprice ask before the appointment: "I can list at any price you choose. My job is to tell you what each price costs you in time and final sale price."

A Full Example CMA Narrative (Written With AI)

A complete example, built with the workflow above. Numbers are fictional; the structure is exactly what you deliver.

The written narrative (leave-behind):

"48 Cedar Lane: 4-bed, 2.5-bath colonial, 2,400 sq ft, quarter acre, 2021 kitchen, original baths. Three most comparable sales within one mile, last six months:

124 Birch Street: $512,000 in March. Nearly identical but with a finished basement, so adjusted down $18,000 to $494,000 against your home.

77 Willow Drive: $478,000 in January. Backs to the main road (about $12,000 here) with an original kitchen (roughly $15,000 below yours). Adjusted: $505,000 for your home.

9 Spruce Court: the low sale at $465,000 in February. Estate sale, dated interior, priced for a fast close. It sets the floor; the circumstances explain the discount.

Adjusted values cluster between $494,000 and $505,000. I recommend $499,900: the heart of the data, priced for strong early activity. Heavy traffic in week one, we hold. Soft activity after two weeks, we revisit."

The spoken version (kitchen table, 90 seconds):

"I kept the three closest matches from six months of nearby sales. The strongest sold for $512,000, but with a finished basement yours lacks, so fairly compared: $494,000. The second sold for $478,000, but it backs to the main road with an original kitchen, so against your home that's really $505,000. The third is the low one at $465,000: an estate sale, dated inside, priced to move. It sets the floor, not the ceiling. Your range is $494,000 to $505,000; I'm recommending $499,900, priced to drive traffic in the first two weeks, when it matters most."

Every number has a reason, the low comp is addressed head-on, the price comes with a plan. AI drafted it in a minute from a comp grid; the agent chose the comps, set the adjustments, approved every word.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can AI write my whole CMA for me?

No, and you should not want it to. Choosing comps and setting adjustments is licensed valuation judgment. AI comparative market analysis (CMA) narrative writing covers the explanation layer, not the valuation layer. You appraise. It explains.

What do I say when the seller shows me a higher online estimate?

Online estimates average nearby sales without walking through the house. They cannot see your updated kitchen or the road noise at the comp. Have AI draft a respectful one-paragraph reply, then show your work.

How long should the written narrative be?

One page, about 200 words. The spoken version runs about 90 seconds. Past two minutes on price, you have lost them.

Will sellers think a robot priced their home?

Only if you read the draft word for word. Edit it into your voice. Sellers care that the explanation makes sense and that you stand behind the number.
